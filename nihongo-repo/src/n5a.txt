== Hiragana (平仮名)
Japanese writing uses three main scripts: **hiragana**, **katakana** and **kanji**. Hiragana and katakana are *syllabaries* (each character is a syllable); kanji are characters that carry meaning.
Hiragana is used all the time, especially for the grammatical parts of the language: particles, verb and adjective endings. It is also used to write words whose kanji are rare or that the reader might not know.
Small hiragana are sometimes written above kanji to show how they are read. These are called **furigana** (振り仮名). You see them a lot in manga for young readers, who do not yet know all the common kanji.
Some basic hiragana can take a **diacritic**, a small mark that changes the pronunciation:
li: **dakuten** (濁点), commonly called *ten-ten*: ゛ — か (ka) → が (ga), さ → ざ, た → だ, は → ば
li: **handakuten** (半濁点), commonly called *maru*: ゜ — only on the h-row: は (ha) → ぱ (pa)
fig: diacritics
Other special features of hiragana:
li: a **small っ** doubles the following consonant: ゆっくり (*yukkuri*, slowly).
li: a **small ゃ, ゅ, ょ** can follow a kana ending in *-i* to form a combined sound: き + ゃ = きゃ (*kya*).
li: **long vowels**: う after an *o*-sound, or い after an *e*-sound, lengthens the vowel: のう (*nō*, brain), せんせい (*sensei*, teacher). For a long *a, i, u* you add あ, い, う: おかあさん (*okāsan*), おにいさん, すうがく.

== Katakana (片仮名)
Katakana is the second syllabary. It is used mainly for:
li: **foreign loanwords**, such as コンクール (*konkūru*, contest, from the French *concours*) or コーヒー (*kōhī*, coffee);
li: **foreign names**, such as ジョン・スミス (*Jon Sumisu*, John Smith);
li: **onomatopoeia**, such as ケロケロ (*kero kero*, the croaking of a frog).
Katakana can also be used to make a word stand out, a little like italics. Some native words are therefore often written in katakana even though they are not foreign: ケガ (*kega*, injury), リンゴ (*ringo*, apple), イルカ (*iruka*, dolphin).
Three special signs:
li: ー lengthens the previous vowel: アパート (*apāto*, apartment).
li: a **small ッ** doubles the following consonant: アルファベット (*arufabetto*, alphabet).
li: ・ separates words written in katakana, e.g. first name and surname: ジョン・スミス. It is optional.

== Kanji (漢字)
There is no fixed total number of kanji, but around 2,000 are needed for everyday reading. During the six years of elementary school, Japanese children learn **1,026** kanji, called **gakushū kanji** (学習漢字, "learning kanji").
In junior and senior high school they learn about 1,100 more. Together these make up the **jōyō kanji** (常用漢字, "kanji for daily use"): **2,136** characters in the official list.
A kanji usually has two kinds of reading:
li: the **on-yomi** (音読み), the reading derived from Chinese — often used in compound words: 日本 (*ni-hon*);
li: the **kun-yomi** (訓読み), the native Japanese reading — often used when the kanji stands alone: 日 (*hi*, sun/day).
The kanji in this course are introduced little by little and match the **N5** and **N4** levels of the **JLPT** (Japanese-Language Proficiency Test), the international Japanese exam — the equivalent of TOEFL or IELTS for English. N5 is the easiest level and N1 the hardest.
note: The sign 々 (often called *noma*) is not a hiragana, katakana or kanji: it simply repeats the previous kanji, as in 時々 (*tokidoki*, sometimes) or 人々 (*hitobito*, people).
tip: Every kana and kanji has a fixed **stroke order**. As a general rule, write from **top to bottom** and from **left to right**. Learning the correct order from the start makes your handwriting readable and helps you remember the characters.

== Particles (1)
The basic structure of a Japanese sentence is:
rule: Topic / Subject + Object + Verb
**Particles** are added to this structure. They are short words placed *after* a noun to show its role in the sentence — a bit like prepositions in English, except they come after the word. In this lesson we study four essential ones.
=== 1. は — the topic
は is pronounced **wa** when used as a particle. It marks the **topic** of the sentence: "as for…". There is no exact English equivalent. In the sentence below, the topic is わたし (*watashi*, I), so は goes after it.
ex: わたしはハナです。 | Watashi wa Hana desu. | I am Hana.
=== 2. の — possession and linking nouns
の (*no*) links two nouns and often shows possession, like "'s" or "of". Note the order: the owner comes first.
ex: ローラのほん。 | Rōra no hon. | Laura's book.
ex: にほんごのじゅぎょう。 | Nihongo no jugyō. | The Japanese class.
=== 3. を — the direct object
を is pronounced **o** when used as a particle. It marks the **direct object** of the verb — the thing being eaten, read, watched…
ex: わたしはりんごをたべます。 | Watashi wa ringo o tabemasu. | I eat an apple.
ex: わたしはにほんごのきょうかしょをよみます。 | Watashi wa nihongo no kyōkasho o yomimasu. | I read the Japanese textbook.
=== 4. です — "to be"
です (*desu*) works like the verb "to be". Grammatically it is a **copula** (a word linking the subject to a noun or adjective), not a particle — but in most cases it translates as "is / am / are".
ex: いぬです。 | Inu desu. | It's a dog.
note: です comes directly after the noun: いぬです, not いぬをです. The subject is often left out when it is obvious from context — いぬです simply means "(it) is a dog".
== Introducing yourself
=== 人 and 語
人 is the kanji for "person". On its own it is read ひと (*hito*, kun-yomi). After another word it is usually read じん (*jin*, on-yomi). Put it after the name of a country to say a person from that country.
table: :: Country | Person | Language
- 日本 (にほん) Japan | 日本人 (にほんじん) a Japanese person | 日本語 (にほんご) Japanese
- フランス France | フランス人 (フランスじん) a French person | フランス語 (フランスご) French
- イギリス the UK | イギリス人 a British person | 英語 (えいご) English
語 (*go*) after a country name means "the language of".
=== Useful phrases
ex: こんにちは。わたしのなまえは＿＿です。 | Konnichiwa. Watashi no namae wa ___ desu. | Hello. My name is ___.
ex: わたしは＿＿です。 | Watashi wa ___ desu. | I am ___.
ex: わたしはフランス人です。 | Watashi wa Furansujin desu. | I am French.
ex: よろしくおねがいします。 | Yoroshiku onegaishimasu. | Nice to meet you. / I look forward to working with you.
note: よろしくおねがいします literally asks the other person to "treat you favourably". It ends almost every self-introduction, and is also used when you ask someone for a favour or start working together.

== Particles (2)
=== 1. に — time, destination, "on/into"
に (*ni*) is often translated "at", "to" or "in". It marks a **point in time** and the **destination** or location where something is. With the verbs のる (to get on/ride) and のぼる (to climb), に marks what you get on or climb.
ex: ちかてつにのります。 | Chikatetsu ni norimasu. | I get on the subway.
ex: まいあさ、8時におきます。 | Maiasa, hachi-ji ni okimasu. | I get up at 8 every morning.
note: Relative time words such as まいあさ (every morning), きょう (today) or あした (tomorrow) do **not** take に. Clock times, dates and days of the week do.
=== 2. から / まで — from / until
から (*kara*) means "from" and まで (*made*) means "until / as far as". They mark a starting point and an end point, in space or in time.
ex: いえからぎんこうまで。 | Ie kara ginkō made. | From the house to the bank.
ex: 私はごぜん10時からごご5時まではたらきます。 | Watashi wa gozen jū-ji kara gogo go-ji made hatarakimasu. | I work from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m.
=== 3. へ — direction
へ is pronounced **e** when used as a particle. It indicates **direction** of movement ("towards"). It is typically used with いきます (*ikimasu*, to go), きます (*kimasu*, to come) and かえります (*kaerimasu*, to go back home).
ex: 9時にしごとへいきます。 | Ku-ji ni shigoto e ikimasu. | I go to work at 9.
ex: どこへいきますか。 | Doko e ikimasu ka. | Where are you going?
note: With verbs of movement, へ and に are usually interchangeable. へ focuses on the **direction** of the movement, に on the **destination** you reach. いえにかえります and いえへかえります both mean "I'm going home".
ex: いえにかえります。 | Ie ni kaerimasu. | I'm going (back) home.
=== 4. で — means and place of action
で (*de*) comes after a noun to indicate the **means** or method ("by, with, in") or the **place where an action happens**.
ex: バスでがっこうにいきます。 | Basu de gakkō ni ikimasu. | I go to school by bus.
ex: カードではらいます。 | Kādo de haraimasu. | I'll pay by card.
ex: としょかんでべんきょうします。 | Toshokan de benkyō shimasu. | I study at the library.
warn: に marks where something **is** or **goes**; で marks where something **is done**. としょかんにいます (I am in the library) vs. としょかんでよみます (I read in the library).
=== 5. ね — "isn't it?"
ね (*ne*) is used much more in speech than in writing. At the end of a sentence it softens the statement or seeks agreement ("isn't it?", "right?"). At the start of a sentence (ねえ) it gets someone's attention.
ex: ねえ、聞こえますか。 | Nē, kikoemasu ka. | Hey, can you hear me?
ex: きれいですね。 | Kirei desu ne. | It's pretty, isn't it?
=== 6. よ — emphasis
よ (*yo*) is used almost only in speech. At the end of a sentence it asserts something strongly or tells the listener something new — like "I tell you!".
ex: 日本語はかんたんですよ。 | Nihongo wa kantan desu yo. | Japanese is easy, you know!

== Particles (3)
=== 1. か — "or"
Between two nouns, か (*ka*) means "or".
ex: あおいかあかいか。 | Aoi ka akai ka. | Blue or red?
ex: コーヒーかこうちゃをのみます。 | Kōhī ka kōcha o nomimasu. | I drink coffee or tea.
=== 2. と — "and", "with"
と (*to*) joins nouns ("and") in a **complete** list, or marks the person you do something with ("with").
ex: ともだちとカラオケへいきます。 | Tomodachi to karaoke e ikimasu. | I go to karaoke with friends.
ex: しろとくろ。 | Shiro to kuro. | White and black.
=== 3. も — "also", "(not) either"
も (*mo*) expresses similarity or addition. It replaces は, が or を. In a positive sentence it means "also / too"; in a negative sentence, "not… either".
ex: 私も17歳です。 | Watashi mo jūnana-sai desu. | I'm 17 too.
ex: 私も行きません。 | Watashi mo ikimasen. | I'm not going either.
note: も can also be added after other particles: に**も**, から**も**, で**も** (とうきょうにもいきます — I'm also going to Tokyo).
=== 4. や / など — "and so on"
や (*ya*) lists a few things as **examples** from a longer, incomplete list ("…and … among other things"). など (*nado*, "etc.") can be added after the last item.
ex: サンドイッチのなかに、パンやハムやチーズがあります。 | Sandoitchi no naka ni, pan ya hamu ya chīzu ga arimasu. | In a sandwich there is bread, ham, cheese and so on.
ex: 2、4、6などはぐうすうです。 | Ni, yon, roku nado wa gūsū desu. | 2, 4, 6, etc. are even numbers.
=== 5. から / ので — "because"
After a clause (a verb, an adjective, or a noun + です/だ), から (*kara*) means "because / so". Don't confuse it with から "from" after a noun (Lesson 3).
ex: きいろですから。 | Kiiro desu kara. | Because it's yellow.
ので (*node*) also means "because", but it sounds softer and more **objective**: it presents the reason as a natural fact. It is preferred in polite requests and explanations. から sounds more personal and assertive. After a noun or な-adjective, use **なので**.
ex: 彼はびょうきなので、かおいろがわるいです。 | Kare wa byōki na node, kaoiro ga warui desu. | He looks pale because he is ill.
=== 6. が — "but" and the subject marker
が (*ga*) after a clause means "**but**", showing contrast between two parts of a sentence.
ex: 日本語は話せますが、中国語は話せません。 | Nihongo wa hanasemasu ga, chūgokugo wa hanasemasen. | I can speak Japanese, but I can't speak Chinese.
が after a noun also marks the **grammatical subject**, especially when the subject is new information or the focus of the sentence, and inside subordinate clauses.
ex: 猫がえさを食べました。 | Neko ga esa o tabemashita. | The cat ate the food. (It was the cat who ate it.)
When you simply describe an event you see happening, the subject usually takes が:
ex: ドアがあきます。 | Doa ga akimasu. | The door opens.
ex: でんきがきえます。 | Denki ga kiemasu. | The light goes out.
tip: Rough rule: は = "as for X…" (the topic, already known). が = "X is the one that…" (new information, focus).
== Pointing at things: こ・そ・あ・ど
fig: kosoado | The こ-, そ- and あ- words depend on distance from speaker and listener.
These **demonstratives** translate as "this / that":
li: これ / この (*kore / kono*): something close to **me** (the speaker).
li: それ / その (*sore / sono*): something close to **you** (the listener).
li: あれ / あの (*are / ano*): something far from **both** of us.
li: どれ / どの (*dore / dono*): which?
これ / それ / あれ stand alone as pronouns and are followed by a particle. この / その / あの must be followed directly by a noun. The same pattern works for places: ここ / そこ / あそこ / どこ (here / there / over there / where?).
ex: これはかさです。 | Kore wa kasa desu. | This is an umbrella.
ex: あの人はジャンです。 | Ano hito wa Jan desu. | That person is Jean.
ex: ぎんこうはどこですか。—そこです。 | Ginkō wa doko desu ka. — Soko desu. | Where is the bank? — It's there (near you).
== Asking questions
=== 1. か at the end of a sentence
か at the **end** of a sentence turns it into a question. No question mark is needed in standard Japanese.
ex: 駅はどこですか。 | Eki wa doko desu ka. | Where is the station?
=== 2. なぜ / なんで — why?
なぜ (*naze*) and なんで (*nande*, more casual) mean "why?". どうして (*dōshite*) is another very common option.
ex: なぜ彼はいつもおくれるのですか。 | Naze kare wa itsumo okureru no desu ka. | Why is he always late?
=== 3. なに / なん — what?
なに / なん (*nani / nan*) mean "what?". Use **なん** before です, before counters (なんじ, what time) and before words starting with *t, d, n*. Use **なに** in most other cases, especially before particles like を and が.
ex: これはなんですか。 | Kore wa nan desu ka. | What is this?
ex: いま、なにをしますか。 | Ima, nani o shimasu ka. | What are you going to do now?
=== 4. かな — "I wonder…"
かな (*kana*) at the end of a casual sentence (verb in plain form) expresses wondering: "I wonder if…".
ex: あめがふるかな。 | Ame ga furu ka na. | I wonder if it will rain.

=== Saying the date
fig: date
A date always goes from the largest unit to the smallest: **year** (年 *nen*), **month** (月 *gatsu*), then **day** (日 *nichi*).
ex: 今日はなんがつなんにちですか。 | Kyō wa nan-gatsu nan-nichi desu ka. | What's the date today?
ex: 今日は2025年9月12日です。 | Kyō wa nisen-nijūgo-nen ku-gatsu jūni-nichi desu. | Today is 12 September 2025.
warn: Days 1–10, 14, 20 and 24 have **special readings**. For the other days, just say the number + にち (*nichi*): 11日 = じゅういちにち, 15日 = じゅうごにち.
table: :: Day | Kana | Rōmaji
- 1日 | ついたち | tsuitachi
- 2日 | ふつか | futsuka
- 3日 | みっか | mikka
- 4日 | よっか | yokka
- 5日 | いつか | itsuka
- 6日 | むいか | muika
- 7日 | なのか | nanoka
- 8日 | ようか | yōka
- 9日 | ここのか | kokonoka
- 10日 | とおか | tōka
- 14日 | じゅうよっか | jūyokka
- 20日 | はつか | hatsuka
- 24日 | にじゅうよっか | nijūyokka
tip: **Practice:** say today's date and your date of birth out loud in Japanese.
== Telling the time
fig: clock
The order is the same as in English: **hour** (時 *ji*), then **minutes** (分 *fun* / *pun*).
ex: いちじ (1時) | ichi-ji | 1 o'clock
ex: にふん (2分) | ni-fun | 2 minutes
ex: きゅうふん (9分) | kyū-fun | 9 minutes
table: Irregular readings :: | Kana | Rōmaji
- 4 o'clock | よじ | yo-ji
- 7 o'clock | しちじ | shichi-ji
- 9 o'clock | くじ | ku-ji
- 1 minute | いっぷん | ippun
- 3 minutes | さんぷん | sanpun
- 4 minutes | よんぷん | yonpun
- 6 minutes | ろっぷん | roppun
- 8 minutes | はっぷん | happun
- 10 minutes | じゅっぷん | juppun
- 20, 30, 40 … minutes | にじゅっぷん、さんじゅっぷん… | nijuppun, sanjuppun…
note: Every multiple of ten minutes follows the 10-minute pattern: さんじゅっぷん, よんじゅっぷん… "Half past" can also be said with 半 (*han*): しちじはん = 7:30.
ex: いま、なんじですか。—いま、ごぜん7時30分です。 | Ima, nan-ji desu ka? — Ima, gozen shichi-ji sanjuppun desu. | What time is it now? — It's 7:30 a.m.
ex: どのぐらいかかりますか。 | Dono gurai kakarimasu ka. | How long does it take?

== Plain (dictionary) form
The **plain form** (also called *dictionary form*) is the form you find in a dictionary. In speech it is used in **casual** conversation. We start with it because it shows the structure of each verb clearly.
Japanese has two regular verb groups, **godan** (五段, "five-row") and **ichidan** (一段, "one-row"), plus only **two irregular verbs**: する (*suru*, to do) and 来る (*kuru*, to come).
fig: verbgroups
=== Ichidan verbs
Ichidan verbs are the easiest: their conjugation is simple and regular. Their dictionary form ends in **-いる** or **-える** (i.e. *-iru* / *-eru*). You remove the final る to get a **fixed stem**, then add the ending you need.
Example: 食べます (*tabemasu*, to eat) is ichidan. Its plain form is 食べる; its stem is 食べ, which never changes:
ex: 食べない | tabenai | don't eat (negative)
ex: 食べた | tabeta | ate (past)
table: Common ichidan verbs :: Kanji | Kana | English
- 食べる | たべる | to eat
- 寝る | ねる | to sleep, go to bed
- 見る | みる | to see, watch
- 出る | でる | to go out, leave
- 生きる | いきる | to live
- 起きる | おきる | to get up
warn: **"Fake ichidan" verbs.** These end in -iru / -eru but are actually **godan**: 要る (いる, to need), 帰る (かえる, to go home), 切る (きる, to cut), 喋る (しゃべる, to chat), 知る (しる, to know), 入る (はいる, to enter), 走る (はしる, to run).
=== Godan verbs
Godan verbs always end in a **-u sound** (う, つ, る, む, ぶ, ぬ, く, ぐ, す). The final kana changes depending on the conjugation — it moves across the five vowel rows *a, i, u, e, o*, which gives the group its name.
ex: 飲まない | nomanai | don't drink (negative of 飲む)
ex: 飲んだ | nonda | drank (past of 飲む)
table: Common godan verbs :: Kanji | Kana | English
- 待つ | まつ | to wait
- 飲む | のむ | to drink
- 死ぬ | しぬ | to die
- 許す | ゆるす | to forgive
- 思う | おもう | to think
- 働く | はたらく | to work
- 学ぶ | まなぶ | to learn, study
- 選ぶ | えらぶ | to choose
== The negative (ない form)
rule: Ichidan: remove る + ない
ex: 食べる → 食べない | taberu → tabenai | to eat → not to eat
rule: Godan: final -u → -a + ない
table: :: Verb | Negative | English
- 待つ (matsu) | 待たない (matanai) | not wait
- 死ぬ (shinu) | 死なない (shinanai) | not die
- 帰る (kaeru) | 帰らない (kaeranai) | not go home
- 飲む (nomu) | 飲まない (nomanai) | not drink
warn: Verbs ending in **う** become **わ**ない, not あない: 買う (*kau*) → 買わない (*kawanai*), 言う → 言わない. And the verb ある (to exist) has the irregular negative **ない**.
warn: The two irregular verbs: する → **しない**, 来る (くる) → **来ない (こない)**.
== The past (た form)
rule: Ichidan: remove る + た
ex: 食べる → 食べた | taberu → tabeta | ate
For **godan** verbs, the past ending depends on the last kana of the verb. Plain past forms always end in **た** or **だ**. Learn this table well — the て form in Lesson 9 works exactly the same way.
table: Godan past forms :: Verb ending | Past ending | Examples
- う・つ・る | った | 買う (to buy) → 買った・分かる (to understand) → 分かった
- ぬ・ぶ・む | んだ | 遊ぶ (to play) → 遊んだ・飲む (to drink) → 飲んだ
- く | いた | 書く (to write) → 書いた・聞く (to listen) → 聞いた
- ぐ | いだ | 泳ぐ (to swim) → 泳いだ・急ぐ (to hurry) → 急いだ
- す | した | 許す (to forgive) → 許した・貸す (to lend) → 貸した
warn: Exception: 行く (いく, to go) → **行った** (not 行いた). Irregular verbs: する → **した**, 来る → **来た (きた)**.
== The past negative
rule: Ichidan: remove る + なかった
ex: 食べる → 食べなかった | taberu → tabenakatta | didn't eat
rule: Godan: negative form, replace the final い with かった
ex: 待つ → 待たない → 待たなかった | matsu → matanai → matanakatta | didn't wait
ex: 帰る → 帰らない → 帰らなかった | kaeru → kaeranai → kaeranakatta | didn't go home
Irregular: する → しなかった, 来る → 来なかった (こなかった).
== The polite ます form
To speak formally and politely, use the **ます form**: replace the plain ending with ます.
rule: Ichidan: remove る + ます
ex: 食べる → 食べます | taberu → tabemasu | to eat
ex: 寝る → 寝ます | neru → nemasu | to sleep
rule: Godan: final -u → -i + ます
ex: 読む → 読みます | yomu → yomimasu | to read
ex: 書く → 書きます | kaku → kakimasu | to write
Irregular: する → します, 来る → 来ます (きます).
Once you have the ます form, conjugating is easy:
table: :: Form | Ending | Examples
- Present / future | ～ます | たべます・のみます
- Negative | ～ません | たべません・のみません
- Past | ～ました | たべました・のみました
- Past negative | ～ませんでした | たべませんでした・のみませんでした
== です in all its forms
table: :: | Plain (casual) | Polite
- Present | だ | です
- Negative | ではない / じゃない | ではありません / じゃありません
- Past | だった | でした
- Past negative | ではなかった / じゃなかった | ではありませんでした / じゃありませんでした
You will sometimes see **である**. It is a more formal, written form of です / だ, common in books, articles and essays, with a neutral, impersonal tone.
note: Japanese has **no separate future tense**. The present (non-past) form is used for the future too, and context makes it clear: あした行きます = "I will go tomorrow".
